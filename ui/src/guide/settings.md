# Settings

A handful of things you set from the browser: how the UI looks and sends, a notice for every worker, each agent's
standing instructions, your loop settings, reloading your config, and the fun. It also shows, read only, which model
each agent runs on and the skills the runtime finds. It opens from the rail's foot, beside the guide. The theme, the
clock, a plan's text size, the send key, whether attention lists chore findings, your review personas, and the fun are
kept in this browser; everything else canopy reads from its config files, as it always did.

## Theme

**Light**, **Dark**, or **System**, which follows your machine. Every open tab follows along. The button at the right
of the header flips it too, and hovering it offers the palettes of the theme you're looking at, so you can try a few
without leaving the page. The card stays put while you click through them; the other theme's are here.

Each theme also has a palette, picked apart; hover one, here or in the header's card, for a line on what it looks
like before you commit to it. The defaults tint the canvas with the accent's hue: **Lavender** in light,
**Indigo** in dark. Light can also be **Off-white**, a warm paper, a soft green **Sage**, **Komorebi**, a wall in
the sun that gets through a tree, **Koi Pond**, a pond on a clear day, or the light side of
**Solarized**, **Nord**, **Gruvbox**, **Rosé Pine** (its Dawn variant), or **Acme**, in the two tones of Plan 9's
editor. **Pulp**, on either side, is a comic printed in four colors: newsprint cream in light, a night blue in dark,
loud flat section colors on both, and process magenta for the button you actually came to press. Dark can be a plain
**Grey**, a near **Black** for an OLED screen, a deep green **Forest**, a wine-dark
**Plum**, **Starry Skies**, a night blue with stars between the panels, or the dark side of **Solarized**, **Gruvbox**,
or **Monokai**. The background, surfaces, text, and accent change. Schemes with an accent bring it: Nord and Solarized
their blues, Monokai its hot pink, Rosé Pine its dusty one, Acme its dark blue, Gruvbox its purple, since its orange
looked like every run was blocked. The rest get one that suits them: ink blue on Off-white, heather on Sage and in the
Forest alike, wisteria on Komorebi, a water iris's violet on Koi Pond, steel blue on Grey, ultraviolet on Black,
orchid on Plum, and starlight on Starry Skies, since gold stars looked a lot like every run was waiting on you.
Neither green one gets a green accent, for the same reason Gruvbox lost its orange, and the Forest gave up its lake
blue, which kept passing for a ticket. Every status color stays where you learned it. Scheme purists
will notice the bases and accents sit a shade off the spec, so everything stays readable on them.
Nord's dark side didn't make it: moved far enough for that, it was Indigo with a different name. Rosé Pine's and
Dracula's dark sides missed out the same way.

A few palettes bring more than color. Dark **Gruvbox** has the crisp corners of an editor from the nineties, sets every
line in monospace as one would, and menus and cards cast a hard shadow with no blur. **Pulp** takes all of that and
prints it: every panel and speech bubble is outlined and drops a hard shadow onto a page whose far corner fades into
cyan Ben-Day dots, clear wherever there is something to read, each bubble grows a balloon tail from its square corner,
pointing out to its speaker's side, a title casts its section's color, and the primary button sinks into its shadow
when you press it. A section's own page is lettered like a comic's cover, in hand-drawn caps with a cyan plate printed
a hair out of register; a ticket's or a run's title stays in monospace, so it wraps where it always did. The ink goes
on inside the panels too: chips, fields, and the quieter buttons are outlined, and the item you picked in a list lifts
off it onto its own shadow, so you can find it from across the room. At night the line is cream and the shadow an
ultramarine plate, since black ink on a night sky is just more night. The header gets the treatment too: the name
sits on an inked plate tilted like a sound effect, and the strip under it prints each section's block between ink
gutters. One thing does explode: an attention item that clears while you watch goes off in a starburst with a sound
effect, where the other palettes drop a leaf. A run of clears gets one bang, not a fireworks show. **Black** drops the
panels'
outlines, since a step of lightness does the job on an OLED screen, rounds its corners, and lights what floats with its
ultraviolet, because a shadow on black is a shadow nobody sees. The rest cast a soft shadow under what floats. Gruvbox
turns the strip under the header into a stepped status bar and sets the name on a yellow block, and its section colors
fade to Gruvbox's own; Black makes it glow like a neon sign, and turns its section colors all the way up. The others
have a little of the same: Gruvbox's light side matches its dark one, monospace included, Solarized is crisp and flat,
Monokai crisp and monospaced with a neon glow, Acme square-cornered and monospaced, pale yellow panels on a pale cyan
page under one olive rule, the way Plan 9's editor drew them, before anyone thought to ask for a dark mode, Nord's light
side a calm fade through its blues, Grey one steel-blue rule, Off-white panels lying on the page like paper, and Sage,
Rosé Pine, Forest, and Plum round their corners, Plum most of all. **Starry Skies** rounds its corners too, puts a
field of stars in the gaps between panels, never behind anything you have to read, lets them twinkle, the brightest
with a glint, leaves a little nebula low in the sky, hangs a crescent moon in the lower right corner, and sends a
meteor across the open sky every twenty seconds or so. The moon is usually behind a panel, as moons are behind
clouds; a tall window on a short page has the best view. Make a wish; it won't merge your PR, but nothing else will
either. If your system asks for reduced motion, the sky holds still and the meteors stay home. **Komorebi** is the
daytime answer, named for the Japanese word for sunlight coming through leaves: flecks of sun and the soft shadows of
leaves lie on the page between the panels, a bough's shadow sways in the lower right corner, and every so often a
leaf's shadow drifts down the open page. The rail, the header, and a page's title stay clear of all of it. Where
words sit straight on the page rather than in a panel, as this guide's do, a shadow will cross something you read, so
the shadows are faint enough to read through, two of them never add up to a darker one, and muted text and links are
a shade deeper than they would otherwise be. It is also the only palette under which a console called canopy looks
like it is under one. Reduced motion stops the breeze, and the leaves stay on the tree. **Koi Pond** looks down into
a pond: the page is pale water, deeper toward the lower right corner, where lily pads float around an open lily and
a koi lies with its tail under one. The panels float too, each in a ring of light, and the strip under the header is
the waterline. Nets of sunlight lie on the pond's floor and all but hold still. About every two minutes a koi swims
one slow arc along the foot of the window, under the panels, a large one and a small one taking turns, and now and
then a ripple opens there; either may pass under words set straight on the page, as this guide's are, for a second
or two. The rail, the header, and a page's title stay clear of all of it, and the pads keep to the corner this
guide's text leaves free, so a window too narrow to leave one has no pads. The pads are a pale sage and the koi's
gold a pale straw, so neither is mistaken for a run that is going or one that is stuck. The koi under the pad never
moves, which makes it the one thing on the page that is not waiting on you. Reduced motion keeps the visitors away
and the water still. Layout and sizes never change with a palette, so nothing moves when you switch, except under
Gruvbox, Pulp, Monokai, and Acme, where monospace runs wider and a long line wraps a little sooner. Their text is drawn
a step smaller to make up some of the width; every line keeps its height, so nothing shifts up or down. This guide stays
in its usual sans under all four: monospace suits a readout, and a manual set in it reads like one long log line.

## Time

How every time of day on the page reads: **24-hour** (`14:05`, the default) or **12-hour** (`2:05 PM`). **Show time
zone** puts the zone after each one, as your browser abbreviates it (`14:05 EDT`), which settles the question of whose
2 PM it was before anyone has to ask. The line above the switches shows the time now, as it will read. Every time
follows along, the ones you hover for included. Ages (`3m ago`) don't change; minutes are minutes everywhere.

## Plans

How large a plan's text is set: **Tiny**, **Small**, **Normal** (the default, the guide's size), or **Large**, each
named in the size it picks. A sample of a plan under them shows the size you picked, so you see what you're getting
before you open one. The headings come along a step, so a plan keeps its shape at any size. It holds on a ticket's plan
page and in a task's report, which is laid out the same way; the guide stays put. Comments beside a plan follow their
passages as the text grows, so a long plan you'd rather read from across the room loses nothing but some scrolling. Tiny
is for the other direction: the whole plan on one screen, squinting optional but likely.

## Replies

Which key sends what you write: **Enter**, **Ctrl+Enter** (⌘+Enter on a Mac), or **Button only**, if you've ever
sent half a sentence to a worker and would rather not again. It holds in every box you write in to send: a reply, a
note, a comment on a plan, the notes on a review request, a draft or a lesson in your words, and the notice to workers.
Shift+Enter always makes a new line. The key you pick shows on each send button.

## Attention

**Hide chore findings** leaves what your [chores](chores) find off the [attention](attention) list, its count, and
the nav's badge, so the list holds only what needs you. Nothing is dismissed: each chore's page still lists its
findings, and the attention page says how many it left out, with a link back here, so a hidden finding is never
mistaken for none. It's this browser's alone. `canopy status` still lists every finding, and so does the next tab you
sign in from on another machine.

## Reviews

The [personas](tickets#choosing-a-persona) a review you request on the
[reviews page](pull-requests#asking-for-a-review), or from a PR's page, is written in. Check one, say `skeptic`, and
both start with it checked; check several and each request gets a review in each. You can still pick others for one
request, and **Use the default** leaves it to `[personas] reviewer` in your config.

This is yours, not the loop's: it lives in this browser, your config doesn't set it, and the reviews canopy runs on
its own PRs never read it. A persona you prefer that canopy would refuse to run, or one whose file you've since
deleted, is named here and left out of each request rather than refused.

## Notice to workers

Tell every run dispatched from now on about something going on outside the work, such as a CI outage: "Artifactory is
down: CI builds that fetch dependencies will likely fail." Workers, reviews, and chores hear it, and the overseer holds
off sending a resume after a failure the notice explains. This is `canopy notice TEXT --for DURATION`.

Pick how long it lasts, **1 hour**, **4 hours**, or **1 day**, and press **Set notice**. It lapses on its own, and
setting another replaces it. While it's in force, a banner above every page shows it, with **Edit**, **Renew**, and
**Clear**: see [the banner](overview#the-header) for what each does.
A run already going keeps the prompt it started with, so a notice reaches only the runs after it. The CLI can set one
for up to a week, for outages with ambitions.

## Standing instructions

What each agent hears from you on every run. **Open** takes you to a page of its own with all four, one panel each, so
you can read what every agent is told without clicking through. The worker's file reaches every implement, resume,
plan, and task run, the overseer's every tick, and the other two every review and every chore run, after the chore's
own prompt. Each comes after its prompt's own rules, which win where the two disagree. The line under the title says
how many agents hear anything and whether a file is missing or refused; hover the title for the page's rules.

Each agent's file is the one its key names, `paths.worker_instructions` and friends (see
[config files](config-files)), or, with no key, `<agent>.md` in your instructions folder, `instructions/` beside your
`config.toml`: `worker.md`, `overseer.md`, `reviewer.md`, `chore.md`. A run reads that default only while it's there,
so an agent with neither hears nothing. Its panel says so and offers **Create**, which opens an empty editor; **Save**
makes the file, readable by you alone, and **Cancel** thinks better of it. A default can be deleted from its panel's
foot: **Delete** asks once more, then the file goes and the agent's runs hear nothing from then on. A file a key names
is yours to edit here but not to delete, since a run whose named file is gone is refused outright; empty it, or unset
the key. If that key's file doesn't exist yet, the panel says every run of that agent is refused until it does, and
**Save** makes it.

The agents are listed beside the page, or over it in a narrow window, and each is a link to its panel:
`/settings/instructions#reviewer` opens the page there. An agent with no file says `none` in the list, and one whose
runs are refused because of its file says `missing` or `refused`, in red, since a worker that won't start is worth a
glance. A file you've edited and not saved says `unsaved`, and its panel gets a line down its edge.

Each file opens in the same editor you write replies in, toolbar and **Preview** included. The count in the panel's
foot is in bytes, as a run counts them: past 4,096 a run refuses the file outright rather than read half of it, so
**Save** waits until you trim. **Revert** puts back what the file holds. If you or your agent edit the file by hand
instead, `canopy instructions check worker FILE` makes the same count before anything is saved.

Every save and delete keeps the file as it was first: the last 10 versions of each agent's, in this workspace's
`.canopy/`. **Show versions** under a panel lists them, newest first, each with when and before what. **Show** reads
one, and **Restore** puts its text in the editor as an ordinary unsaved edit, so you can look before you **Save**. The
eleventh change drops the oldest, so if a version matters, keep it somewhere a little more permanent than a list of
ten.

A save writes the file in place, and every run started from then on reads the new text; a run already going keeps the
prompt it started with. If the file changed since the page read it, say you edited it in your own editor meanwhile,
the change is refused rather than quietly flattening your other edit. **Read again**, under the title, fetches the file
as it is now and keeps whatever you haven't saved, but an edit you began before the file changed stays refused, and its
panel says so: **Revert** shows the file as it is now, and then you redo yours. Choosing which of you was right is a
job for a person, not a hash. Leave the page with an edit unsaved and it asks first, as
a plan does.

The page never chooses which file: it won't write a key or aim an agent at some other file of yours. That's on purpose.
Anyone holding a session here can put words in every later run's mouth, or delete a default to quiet one, and a new
secret doesn't take either back. If a change shows up that you didn't make, the versions have what was there before.

## Loop settings

**Open** takes you to a page of its own for the numbers you change most. The line under its title is the state of
things: how many settings there are, how many this workspace sets, and how many come from your user config. Hover the
title for the page's rules, and copy the path under it when you'd rather edit the file by hand. The settings come in
six bands. **Capacity** is how many workers, chores, reviews, and tasks run at once, each pool's slots, and how much
you may attach to a ticket. **Attempts** is how many tries a ticket, a plan, or a review's draft gets, and how many
tickets an epic holds. **Timing** comes in three parts, since every one of them is in minutes and you shouldn't have to
guess which: **Pace**, how often the loop ticks and asks the tracker and the connectors what's new; **Waits**, how long
it holds off after a red build, for feedback to go quiet or its checks to finish, and for a PR's head to settle before
its review; and **Timeouts**, how long a run or a slot's setup may take before canopy stops it.

Two rows in those bands aren't counts. Under Capacity, a switch says whether the loop's own PRs get a review before a
person is asked; it goes either way, and what it spends stays under your reviews a day. Under Pace, the shortest gap
between [plan archives](plans) says how often your archive's remote hears from canopy.

**Upkeep** is the housekeeping a tick does in each pool. The first row turns git's own maintenance of a repository's
clone on or off, and the next three say when it runs: past how many packs or loose objects, and how many hours pass
between looks. It only ever runs while no run holds a slot of that pool, so it costs you nothing but a quieter disk.
The last two are for a slot nobody has leased in a while: how many days it sits idle before its idle command runs, and
how long that command may take. The command itself (`[pools] idle_cmd`) is not here, and won't be: what runs on your
machine is an edit to the file.

**Retention & budget** says how many days canopy keeps a run's files, a tick's, the journal's old rows, and a retired
note's text before a tick removes them. Lengthen them as you like, or set 0 to keep that kind forever; shortening stops
at a floor (a week of run files, a month of journal), since the transcript a shorter one would delete is the one that
shows what a run did. If you want last Tuesday gone, the file still takes 1. The log's rotation sits with them: the
size `canopy.log` rotates at and how many rotated files stay. The page will keep more log than stock, or all of it at
0, but never less, since the log is where each save made here is written down. Below them sits the monthly budget the
[spend page](reports#the-spend-page) tracks, with any alerts short of or past it. You can add and drop alerts freely:
the 100% one isn't in the list, so dropping everything still leaves it.

**Guardrails** holds the settings that decide what waits on you and whose words count. You can make tickets wait on
a plan you approve, or stop; give a planner more or less say over its own plan; have workers draft their review
replies for you or post them; turn `trust.rebase` and the commit trailer on or off; and set how many times a flaky
check is rerun. The same band holds what canopy remembers and sends: the memory tiers, session search, and whether the
plan archive takes prompts, conversations, and your comments. All of it goes both ways. The page used to let these
only tighten, on the theory that anything looser deserved a trip to the file; in practice it meant a trip to the file.

Four rows are lists of names: who may answer a worker, whose review a worker acts on unasked, whose comments on an
artifact count, and which agents may publish. Uncheck a name to drop it, or type one into **add a name** and press
Enter to add it. The config loader still has opinions, so a publishing agent it has never heard of is refused on save.
One caution survives from the old rule, as advice rather than a lock: a name on these lists steers your workers, and a
save outlives the sign-in secret. If a name shows up that you didn't add, the log has who changed what, old value and
new.

Each row's chip says where its value comes from, `this workspace`, `user`, `workspace`, or `default` (hover it, or
the page's title, for what each means), and the line beside it says how far the page will take it. Units sit after the
field, as on the rest of Settings, so a label says what a value is and the field says in what. Counts, paces,
timeouts, and the budget take whatever the config file would: the page used to stop them at ceilings of its own,
which mostly stopped you. Only retentions stop at a floor (a week of run files), since a deleted transcript doesn't
come back. Type under one, or something that isn't a whole number, and the field turns red and says why under it as
you type, and **Save** waits until you fix it.
Repositories get a count of their own only when you pick one, so a workspace of fifty repositories shows the two that
differ.

If you know the key, or a word of its label, type it in the filter at the top: `flaky` leaves the one row about
flaky checks, and the address keeps it, so `/settings/limits?q=flaky` is a link to exactly that. Anything you've
changed and not saved stays in view whatever you type, so the filter can't hide a change from you. **Escape** empties
it.

The bands are listed beside the page, or over it in a narrow window, each with how many of its settings this
workspace sets and how many you've changed and not saved yet, so you can see where you've customized things without
scrolling four screens. Every row is a link to itself: `/settings/limits#policy.max_resume_attempts` opens the page at
that row and lights it for a moment, which is handy for a note to your future self.

A setting you've moved off its default says what the default is, so you can tell how far from stock you've wandered,
and **reset** puts the field back. It's an ordinary change: it shows in the bar and waits for **Save** like any other.

Change something and its row gets a line down its edge and says what it was, and a bar appears at the foot listing
each change as `key: old → new`.

**Save** writes `.canopy/config.toml`, this workspace's own file, editing it in place so your comments and layout stay,
then reloads the loop as **Reload** below does. It never touches `canopy-workspace.toml` or
`~/.config/canopy/config.toml`; a value that came from the latter is copied into the workspace's file, where it wins for
this workspace alone. The file as it was is kept beside it as `config.toml.bak`, so undoing a save is a `mv` and a
Reload. If the file changed since the page read it, the save is refused rather than guessing which of you meant it.

## Models

Which model each agent runs on, as `[runtime.models]` chooses it: a row each for the worker (both workers, and a
task's runs), the planner, the reviewer, the overseer, and `chore`, which covers every chore without a model of its
own, then a row for each chore. Each row names the model at the variant it runs at, `provider/model@high`, as the file
spells it, or says `runtime default` when nothing sets one and the runtime picks, which is a choice, just not yours.
A chore with a `model` in its own table is marked **own model**; one whose command runs an agent of your own names
that agent instead, since its own definition chooses (see [chores](chores#setting-one-up)).

```toml
[runtime.models]
worker = "anthropic/claude-sonnet-4-5"
reviewer = "openai/gpt-5@high"
chore = "anthropic/claude-haiku-4-5"
```

The panel only reads. The line under it names the file to edit: the user file serves every workspace, and
`.canopy/config.toml` this one alone; the workspace file can't choose a model, since a model is what your spend buys
and which provider sees the code. It shows what the loop runs on, so an edit appears after **Reload**, and applies from
each agent's next run; a run already going keeps its model. One ticket's override is set on
[its page](tickets#choosing-a-model) and never shows here. **Show the runtime's models** lists what you can name, as
`canopy models` prints it, less what `[runtime] hide_models` hides (see [choosing a model](tickets#choosing-a-model)),
read only when you open it, since asking starts the runtime; canopy then keeps that list for five minutes, or until the
loop reads its config again.

## Skills

Every skill the runtime finds for canopy's agents, on a page of its own: **Open** on the **Skills** panel. Each row
names the skill, says where its file is, and gives what its front matter says it is for, which is all an agent reads
before deciding to load it, so a vague one is a skill nobody loads. **Show SKILL.md** opens the instructions under
the row, as text.

Where a file is, on its chip:

- **canopy**: shipped with canopy, in the snapshot of its definitions the runtime reads. Edit it in canopy, not there.
- **workspace**: in the workspace's own runtime config, under its root.
- **home**: yours, in your home. Every session of yours loads it, canopy's or not.
- **built-in**: the runtime's own.
- **other**: anywhere else.

The runtime is asked as a run asks it, in the workspace root, so a skill a repository keeps for itself is missing here;
its runs, started in its worktrees, still get it. Every skill an agent is offered costs each of its model calls,
loaded or not, so one an agent never uses is cheaper denied to it, by name, in your runtime config. Which agent gets
which is `canopy agents`, which asks the runtime once for each agent and takes its time about it. Which agent was
offered each skill over the last days, how often it loaded it, and what its front matter cost is the
[Skills page under Reports](reports#the-skills-page), which reads what the runs recorded and asks nothing.

The page asks the runtime for its skills alone, once, when it opens, not on a timer; **Read again** asks afresh after
you've added one. The filter narrows the list by name or description. Nothing here changes a skill: add, edit, or deny
one in the runtime's own files.

## Config

The loop reads your workspace's `canopy-workspace.toml` and your `config.toml` when it starts. **Reload** reads them
again, as `kill -HUP` on the loop does; a tick that's running finishes first. It says what changed, and which changes
wait for a restart. A file that doesn't load changes nothing, and the error names its file and key, so a typo costs you
a fix, not a loop. [Config files](config-files) lists every key.
